## What shared web hosting actually is (and who it fits)

Shared hosting puts your website on one physical server shared with other customers, and everyone draws from the same pool of CPU, RAM, and disk I/O. That's the whole trick behind the price: nobody pays for the whole machine, so nobody pays much.

For most first sites this is exactly right. Blogs, portfolios, small business brochure sites, and low-traffic stores run fine on a shared plan — WordPress included. Problems start when a site outgrows its slice: real traffic spikes, an online store with steady checkout volume, or an app that needs background processes. And because you share the server, hosts enforce resource caps so neighbors can't strangle each other. HostGator's fine print, for example, states that accounts may not use 25% or more of system resources for longer than 90 seconds — even on plans marketed as "unmetered."

There's nothing shady about those caps. They're how shared hosting stays cheap. You just want to know them before you buy, not after your site throws a resource-limit error.

## Shared hosting limits that live in the fine print

The specs that actually decide whether your site runs well usually aren't in the comparison table — they're in the footnotes. Check these four on any host before paying:

1. **Inode (file) limits.** Namecheap caps Stellar and Stellar Plus at 300,000 files — and "files" means everything: images, emails, cache, folders. Photo-heavy blogs hit this sooner than you'd expect.
2. **CPU usage clauses.** HostGator's 25%-for-90-seconds rule is the concrete example. If a host doesn't publish a resource clause, ask support before buying.
3. **Soft traffic caps.** Bluehost labels Starter as "ideal for 40K visits/mo." That's not a hard cutoff, but pushing well past it invites throttling or an upsell.
4. **Email limits.** Hostinger's Premium plan includes just 2 mailboxes per website, free for the first year only; Namecheap includes unlimited mailboxes. If your business runs on domain email, this one line can decide the purchase.

Backup frequency deserves a glance too: Hostinger's Premium does weekly backups while its higher tiers do daily, and Bluehost's plans do weekly. Daily automated backups are the safer default for anything you'd hate to rebuild from scratch.
